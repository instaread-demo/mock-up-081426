MVD: New York, New York 4K

There’s a certain point in Martin Scorsese’s 1977 New York, New York where it could be written off as a modestly enjoyable curiosity of amplified artifice, the occasional indelible image and a more danceable dissolution of decorum per Scorsese’s normal. But then the intersection of improvisational jazz and emotions clicks in such a comprehensive and cohesive way that it’s easy to understand why Scorsese fell into such a drug-fueled depression when it died at the box office.

The film’s final hour — the one in which the studio tinkered quite a bit — becomes a magnetic malformation of any comfort in the peppy melodies it provides, made human and horrifying by Robert De Niro and Liza Minnelli’s performances as Jimmy and Francine, a saxophonist and singer making music and love together. (A sequence in a car after Jimmy literally plays Francine off the stage is among one of Scorsese’s more upsetting moments.)

It’s here that the movie obliterates the shallow observation that these two particular people wouldn’t have wound up together. Any number of smart, talented people wind up crushed beneath the heel of someone they commit to against all common sense. And the climax, epilogue and coda here all establish that neither one of them will really escape the other — not with all those anxieties and echoes stacked like nesting dolls in their artistic endeavors. Seen in hindsight, the what-if fantasy of La La Land owes itself more to the trappings of the form — as the “Happy Endings” sequence here rousts more emotional turbulence and subtextual violence.

Minnelli also lets us see Francine’s lingering delusions even in the most lavish musical numbers, the toll that day-to-day drudgery with Jimmy exacts upon her. Even after a few minutes away from the film, De Niro’s return finds him so much more calcified. The name of Jimmy’s club, too, echoing one of his unnervingly desperate ploys to get in Francine’s pants, also looms large over his head. You could say The Major Chord has been eradicated as an ethos. But that would suggest Jimmy could muster anything close to mental structure or order. (In some ways, this feels like De Niro’s dry run for his more physically destructive Dwight in This Boy’s Life 16 years later.)

The third act’s musical numbers are all visual showstoppers, yes, but in a purposefully plastic way. It’s like watching Scorsese pop the balloon with a harpoon. The final piece, which takes its title from the movie and the song, hardly feels cathartic. That song itself has become its own empty, performative gesture of self-improvement. There’s no sense that the victory of Francine’s final choice is permanent, either. Indeed, we train our eyes to the spotlight, the swoon, the dance. But the messier steps in the shadows? Those are the ones we often take. 

New York, New York remains one hell of a movie, and the MVD Marquee Collection’s new 4K Ultra HD Blu-ray remaster captures it in all of its splendor. The disc features the film’s 163-minute expanded cut, newly restored from a 16-bit 4K scan of the original camera negative and presented in its original 1.66:1 aspect ratio with an HDR grade. The colors and details pop very nicely, and although the anti-grain crowd may quibble, this is a strong transfer overall. Meanwhile, it comes with a DTS-HD MA 5.1 lossless surround track and a lossless LPCM 2.0 stereo track. Either sounds good, although the film’s conscious audio effort to evoke the era it’s portraying keeps things largely confined to the front section of the soundscape.

Special features have been ported over from previous releases, including an introduction to the film from Scorsese and a feature-length audio commentary from the early 1990s featuring him and film critic Carrie Rickey (separately recorded and edited together). 

The included Blu-ray also offers the 136-minute international version of the film and additional features like: 19 minutes of alternate takes and deleted scenes, including a different ending; The New York, New York Stories (Parts One and Two), a roughly hourlong featurette that tracks the film’s journey to the screen, noting the irony that the title song wasn’t nominated for an Oscar and wasn’t even a hit until Frank Sinatra recorded it; Liza on New York, New York, a 2005 interview with the actress about the film; select-scene commentary from cinematographer László Kovács on Jimmy’s introduction, Jimmy meeting Francine, the record executive meeting, and the number “But the World Goes Round”; a theatrical teaser; a theatrical trailer; a photo gallery; and reversible artwork.
